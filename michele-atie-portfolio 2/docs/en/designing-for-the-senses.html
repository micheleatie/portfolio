Designing for the Senses

How interiors shape perception and well-being

By Michèle Atié · March 8, 2025

The design of interior spaces has long focused on function and visual aesthetics, but research reminds us that human experience is multi-sensory and shaped by how we perceive and feel space. Designing for the senses means paying attention to how environments stimulate not only vision, but also emotional, physiological, and cognitive responses.

Light beyond the visible

Among the many sensory inputs at play, light is one of the most influential parameters. It structures how we see, orient ourselves, and interpret spatial qualities. But beyond its visual function, light affects our well-being: it modulates circadian rhythms, contributes to hormonal balance, and influences our mood. The use of natural or artificial light — its intensity, color temperature, and interaction with surfaces — plays a role in shaping the atmosphere of a space.

Recent studies show that the spectral qualities of light and the color composition of interior surfaces impact spatial ambiance and physiological responses. For example, certain surface hues (like greens and blues) and finishes can modify the Equivalent Melanopic Lux (EML) — a metric linked to non-visual effects of light on the human body. These factors are relevant in extreme lighting environments, where daylight is rare or seasonal. In these contexts, biophilic lighting strategies — those that mimic or evoke natural conditions — can support psychological resilience and comfort.

Evoking natural conditions

Far from being limited to plants or greenery, biophilic design includes patterns of light and shadow and dynamic visual stimuli, all of which echo natural environments. When implemented thoughtfully, these elements can evoke a sense of calm, safety, and vitality.

One promising approach involves the integration of light and color patterns that mimic natural cycles. For example, using warm hues and soft contrast in the morning or evening, and cooler, more diffuse light during midday, supports users’ circadian alignment and fosters psychological comfort. In experimental settings, such lighting scenarios have shown potential to enhance cognitive performance and emotional stability, particularly in isolated or monotonous environments.

In addition, surface finishes and color configurations can be selected to evoke familiar or comforting sensory cues, such as wood textures or soft matte reflections. These material choices contribute not only to visual ambiance but also to a sensory engagement — often described as a sense of “presence” or “belonging” in the space.

Incorporating such strategies during early design stages allows designers to evaluate how interiors may feel and function.
