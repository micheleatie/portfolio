Neuroarchitecture

How spaces influence our experience

By Michèle Atié · February 7, 2025

What is neuroarchitecture?

Neuroarchitecture is the intersection of architectural design and neuroscience, analyzing how spatial environments influence cognitive functions, emotions, behavior, and well-being. Architectural choices—such as lighting, color, scale, and spatial organization—have a significant impact on mental health.

The concept of neuroarchitecture emerged from an observation made by Jonas Salk, who believed that his stay in a monastery in Assisi enhanced his creativity and problem-solving abilities. Later, in 2003, the Academy of Neuroscience for Architecture (ANFA) officially established this research field, aiming to understand how spatial environments affect human perception and experience.

Neuroarchitecture is applied in urban design and residential environments, but it plays a crucial role in healthcare, education, and workspaces. In healthcare, well-designed hospitals with access to natural light and calming spaces support patient recovery. In education, thoughtful daylight exposure and spatial organization enhance students’ performance. In workplace design, strategic office layouts foster collaboration and productivity.

Key dimensions

  • Physical: facilitating movement and accessibility.
  • Cognitive: enhancing memory, spatial orientation, and learning.
  • Sensorial: creating multisensory experiences to evoke positive emotions.
  • Psychological: balancing calming and stimulating spaces.

Research has shown that positive environmental stimuli activate multiple regions of the brain, including the prefrontal cortex, which plays a crucial role in decision-making, attention, and emotional regulation. This suggests that the spaces we inhabit are not merely passive backgrounds to our lives but active elements that shape our cognitive and emotional states.

Understanding the parameters and their limits

It is essential to study in detail the parameters influencing neuroarchitecture’s impact, examining how specific design elements affect the brain, in what ways, and within which contexts. Just as we invest significant effort into understanding nutrition and physical health, ensuring that we consume the right foods and engage in exercise to maintain well-being, we should also focus on the impact of our built environment on mental and emotional health. After all, we spend the vast majority of our lives inside architectural spaces—whether homes, offices, hospitals, or public areas—yet we often underestimate their psychological and physiological effects.

In an increasingly stressful world, where mental health issues and cognitive overload are growing concerns, it is crucial to explore how we can replace stressful environments with positive, stimulating, and restorative spaces. Just as poor diet leads to chronic illness, poorly designed environments may contribute to stress, anxiety, and decreased cognitive function.
